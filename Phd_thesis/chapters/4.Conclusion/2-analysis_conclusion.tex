\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:analysis_results}

The findings summarized above provide strong evidence validating the central hypothesis of this thesis. The demonstrated results show that enhancing \ac{MPC} with synergistic advancements in data-driven modeling, entanglement-aware planning, and perception-aware control indeed leads to superior performance in complex marine robotic tasks.

The research effectively addressed the key challenges initially identified. The simulation-reality gap was narrowed by the development of \textit{UNav-Sim}, facilitating more trustworthy validation. The detrimental effects of environmental uncertainty and model mismatch on \ac{MPC} performance were significantly mitigated through the adaptive learning capabilities of \textit{DF-GP} and the improved predictive power of \textit{PINC}, resulting in enhanced robustness and tracking precision. The critical mission-safety tradeoffs, particularly tether entanglement for \acp{ROV}, were directly managed by the \textit{REACT} framework, enabling safe operation in previously inaccessible or hazardous scenarios. Finally, the perception-control decoupling issue was tackled by the \textit{VT-NMPC} approach, demonstrating how task objectives can be integrated into the controller for improved mission effectiveness.

The synergy between these advancements is crucial. More accurate and adaptive models derived from data-driven techniques allow the \ac{MPC} to operate more effectively. Tether-aware planners like \textit{REACT} ensure this enhanced control respects operational constraints, while perception-aware formulations guide the robot to maximize task success. Validating these integrated systems is made feasible through realistic simulation. Therefore, the collective evidence confirms that this multi-faceted approach, enhancing \ac{MPC} from different angles, yields autonomous systems that are safer, more robust, and more capable than those based on standard techniques, particularly for underwater inspection missions.

\subsection{Limitations}

While this thesis has made progress toward its objectives, several limitations remain that offer opportunities for future research.

Firstly, the simulation environment \textit{UNav-Sim} currently lacks implementations of several underwater sensor modalities that are critical for broader autonomy applications, such as advanced sonar types, acoustic communication models, and other environmental perception sensors. Expanding the sensor suite within \textit{UNav-Sim} would enable more comprehensive algorithm validation and support a wider range of marine robotic applications.

Secondly, the planning frameworks developed, such as \textit{REACT}, rely on the assumption of a static and well-known environment. While this assumption is reasonable for many offshore installations and controlled inspection scenarios, many real-world marine environments can be dynamic, with unexpected changes or moving obstacles. Extending these planners to handle uncertain, dynamic environments remains an important challenge. Additionally, \textit{REACT} introduces a minor computational overhead due to tether model computations and replanning searches, which can be a disadvantage in scenarios where computational resources are limited.

Thirdly, the learning-based methods developed in this thesis, including \textit{PINC} and \textit{DF-GP}, while demonstrating strong empirical performance improvements, do not yet provide formal theoretical guarantees for stability, convergence, or robustness. This lack of theoretical underpinning poses challenges for certifying these methods in safety-critical marine operations. Future work could focus on establishing such guarantees or integrating these learning methods within frameworks that ensure safe operation.

Addressing these limitations will be critical for further advancing the robustness, reliability, and applicability of autonomous marine robotic systems in complex, real-world settings.
